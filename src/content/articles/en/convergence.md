Money is becoming software. For most of financial history, value moved through institutions, and software was the interface laid on top: a screen for a bank account, a form for a loan, a terminal for a card. That arrangement is inverting. Accounts, payments, assets and credit decisions are turning into code that other code can call.

We invest in fintech, blockchain and AI, but we do not see them as three separate bets. We see one shift viewed from three angles. Our thesis is that the three now reinforce each other and that the boundaries between them are dissolving. We believe the most durable companies of the coming era will be built where they meet, even though the best of them will start somewhere much narrower.

## Three shifts, one direction

The first shift is **financial services distributed as software**. Fintech showed that a bank is a bundle of capabilities, such as managing balances, moving money, verifying identity and underwriting credit, and that each one can be exposed through an interface and embedded wherever customers already work. A lending offer appears inside accounting software. A business account opens inside a marketplace. The lesson of this shift is that distribution and design win, and that compliance is part of the product rather than a department beside it.

The second shift is **a neutral, programmable settlement layer**. Bitcoin showed that a network could record and settle transfers of value without a central operator. Ethereum showed that a ledger of this kind could also run general-purpose programs, so the terms of an agreement execute in the same place the money moves. *Neutral* matters as much as *programmable*. No single company owns the ledger, anyone can build on it, and an asset issued in one application can move to another without asking permission.

The third shift is **automation and judgment at machine speed**. AI models can read a contract, reconcile a ledger, assess a borrower, flag a suspicious transfer and answer a customer, continuously and without a queue. Increasingly, they can also act, taking a sequence of steps toward a goal and calling tools and services along the way. Work that once required a back office is becoming software that runs 24/7.

Each shift matters on its own. Together, they point in one direction: toward money and financial judgment that live inside software, move at the speed of software and can be combined like software.

## Each shift unlocks the others

The case for convergence is not that three technologies happen to be fashionable at the same moment. It is that each one removes a constraint on the other two. Three examples show the mechanism.

**AI agents need programmable money they can safely keep and move.** An agent that books travel, pays for data or rents computing power on behalf of a business has to transact. Card rails assume a human at checkout, with fraud models and dispute processes built around human behavior. Programmable money lets the owner set policy at the level of the money itself: a spending limit, a list of approved counterparties, a time window, a second approval above a set amount. The agent does not need to be trusted completely, because the wallet enforces the rules.

**Stablecoins need distribution and compliance that fintech already knows how to build.** A digital dollar on a public ledger is only useful if people and businesses can move into it and out of it, account for it and use it within the rules that govern money. That requires onboarding, identity checks, sanctions screening, fraud controls, licensing, customer support and dependable connections to the banking system. Fintech companies have spent years building exactly these capabilities. The settlement layer is new. The hard work of reaching customers and staying compliant is not.

**Tokenized assets need intelligent risk management, servicing and compliance.** Putting a loan, a bond or a fund share on a blockchain does not service it. Someone still has to collect payments, monitor collateral, process corporate actions, confirm that each new owner is eligible and report to regulators. Much of that work is heavy on documents and judgment, which is the kind of work models increasingly do well. We believe AI is what will make servicing cheap enough for small, fractional positions to be economical.

Follow any one of these loops and you arrive at the other two. Agents need programmable money, and programmable money needs compliant distribution. Tokenized assets need automated servicing, and automated servicing needs data and judgment. Each category's bottleneck is another category's strength.

> Each of the three removes a constraint on the other two. That is the difference between a trend and a convergence.

## The category boundaries are dissolving

Consider a hypothetical company that helps internet businesses manage cash. It keeps client balances in stablecoins so money can move across borders at any hour. It connects to banks, payroll providers and accounting systems, so those balances fit how a finance team already works. It uses models to forecast cash needs, route each payment along the cheapest compliant path and explain every decision to an auditor. Is it a fintech company, a blockchain company or an AI company?

The honest answer is all three, and we expect the question to matter less over time.

Many institutions have not caught up. Banks often run these capabilities in separate divisions. Regulators often supervise them under separate regimes. Many investors still judge them with separate playbooks. That lag is an opportunity. Founders who understand all three can see designs that specialists miss. They can make the critical trade-offs, such as which part of a flow belongs on a ledger and which belongs in a bank account, that a single-discipline team tends to get wrong.

We believe the most durable companies will sit at the intersections because that is where the hardest problems are, and hard problems are where advantages compound. A license takes time and discipline to earn. Risk data accumulates with every transaction. Integrations with banks and enterprises are slow to build and painful to replace. Trust, once earned in financial services, is sticky. None of these advantages comes from a single technology. All of them come from combining the three well.

## Start with one sharp wedge

There is a trap in this argument. A pitch that leads with fintech, blockchain and AI all at once is usually a warning sign, not a strength. Convergence describes where the strongest companies end up. It rarely describes where they start.

In our experience, the companies that reach the intersections begin with one painful problem for one specific customer, solved decisively better than the alternatives. A narrow wedge is easier to win, easier to explain and easier to measure. It also earns the trust, data and distribution a company needs to expand.

Imagine a team that starts with payouts to freelancers in markets where bank transfers are slow and costly. The first product does one thing: it gets money to the freelancer quickly, using stablecoins for settlement and local partners for cash-out. Once that works, the company has funded balances, a compliance stack and a stream of transaction data. Treasury tools, currency conversion and working-capital credit become natural next steps, and models trained on that data make each step better. The wedge was payouts. The destination is a financial operating system for a whole class of workers.

We ask founders three questions about their wedge:

1. Who is the first customer, and what do they do today without you?
2. Why is your approach decisively better for that customer, rather than marginally better?
3. If you removed the blockchain or the model, would the product still work? If it would, that technology is decoration, not foundation.

The best wedges are narrow enough to win and positioned so that winning opens the door to adjacent categories.

## What could slow this down

We are confident in this view, but it comes with real caveats.

In our view, regulation is the biggest variable. Regulators in several large markets are writing rules for stablecoins, digital assets and the use of AI in financial decisions, and those rules do not always agree. A product that is compliant in one market may need to be redesigned for the next.

Security is next. Programmable money also means programmable failure. A flawed contract or a compromised key can move funds irreversibly, and an agent with spending authority is a new kind of attack surface. Accountability follows. When a model declines a loan or an agent makes a payment, someone has to explain the decision and stand behind it, and in financial services that someone must be identifiable.

We do not see these as reasons to wait. We see them as the work. We expect the founders who treat compliance, security and explainability as product features, rather than costs to minimize, to earn the trust of customers and regulators first. In converging markets, that trust is the scarcest resource.

## How we work

We invest in companies building across fintech, blockchain and AI, and we pay particular attention to teams whose wedge leads toward the intersections. In our view, capital alone is rarely the binding constraint for these companies. The harder constraint is the number of demanding disciplines a small team must master at once.
