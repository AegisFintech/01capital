A pitch deck has one job: to make a smart, busy reader want the next conversation. It does not need to answer every question. It needs to show that you understand a real problem better than anyone else, that you are making progress on it, and that your team is the right one to solve it.

We read decks across fintech, blockchain and AI, and the strongest share a common shape. This guide follows that shape in roughly the order we read, explains what we look for at each step and ends with the mistakes that most often get in the way. Very little of it is about design. Clear thinking matters far more than polish.

## The one-sentence test

Before you build a single slide, write one sentence that says what your company does, for whom, and why it matters. If you cannot write it, no amount of design will rescue the deck.

A good sentence is concrete enough to picture. Compare *we are building the future of finance with AI* with *we help small importers pay overseas suppliers in stablecoins, without opening a foreign bank account*. The first could describe almost anyone. The second describes one company, and it invites the right questions: which payment corridors, which compliance obligations and how you reach importers.

Put that sentence on the first slide, under your company name. It frames everything that follows, and it is the line a reader will repeat when passing your deck to colleagues.

## The story: problem, timing and product

### The problem, and exactly who has it

Name the person who feels the pain, by role or circumstance, and describe it in their words. *Payments are broken* is not a problem statement. *Finance teams at mid-sized exporters wait days for cross-border settlement and cannot predict the fees* is.

Then show evidence that the problem is real and costly: customer interviews, workarounds people already pay for, budgets that already exist. The narrower and more specific your first customer, the better. The way into a large market is usually a narrow door.

### Why now

Most good ideas have been attempted before. Tell us what has changed that makes this one work now: a new technology, a cost that has fallen far enough to matter, a shift in regulation or a change in customer behavior.

In our pillars, a strong answer is usually concrete: a model that became good enough for a specific task, settlement rails that made a slow process fast, or infrastructure that made an expensive operation cheap. If nothing has changed, explain why earlier attempts failed and why yours will not.

### The product, shown rather than described

Show the product. Screenshots, a short demo video or a live link beat paragraphs of description. Walk through the core workflow from the user's point of view: what they do, what happens and what they get.

If the product is early, show the prototype and say plainly what is built and what is planned. We would much rather see an honest prototype than a mockup presented as finished software. For AI products, show output on real inputs, including where it fails. For blockchain products, show what the user experiences, not only the architecture diagram.

## The evidence: traction, market and model

### Traction, presented honestly

Traction is the strongest argument in any deck and the easiest to undermine. Show the metrics that reflect real value, such as revenue, active usage, retention and pilots that convert into contracts. Define each one, show the trend over time and label the axes.

Honesty compounds here. If growth came from a single customer, a paid campaign or an incentive program, say so. If a metric fell, explain why. Before revenue, real evidence of demand still counts: design partners using the product every week, signed letters of intent, a waitlist that actually engages.

> We would rather hear about a weak metric from you than discover it in diligence.

### A market built from the bottom up

Skip the slide with a giant number for an entire industry. It tells us nothing about your business. Build the market from the bottom up instead: how many customers of your first type exist, what each would pay you and how you will reach them.

Then show how the opportunity grows as the product does, into adjacent segments, geographies and use cases. A smaller estimate that is credible persuades far more than an enormous one that is borrowed.

### The business model, and the token model if there is one

Say who pays, for what, how often and what it costs you to deliver. Show the unit economics you have, even if they are early, and explain what has to be true for them to improve.

If your project has a token, explain the business first and the token second. Tell us what job the token does in the system, who needs it and why, and how value flows between users, operators and the company. Then apply the test that matters most: the business must work without relying on the token price. Model revenue, incentives and runway in stable terms, and show that they stand on their own.

## The context: competition, team, regulation and the raise

### Competition, presented honestly

Never write *we have no competitors*. It suggests either that you have not looked or that there is no market. Every customer is doing something about the problem today, even if it is a spreadsheet, a manual process or simply living with it. That is your real competition.

Skip the chart where you sit alone in the top right corner. Describe the alternatives fairly, say where each is strong and explain the specific reason customers choose you. Point to advantages that compound over time, such as proprietary data, network effects, distribution or regulatory approvals, and be clear about which you have today and which you are still building.

### The team: why you

We back people first. Tell us why this team is the one to solve this problem: the domain experience that lets you see what others miss, the technical depth to build it and evidence that you ship quickly. Show what each founder has built or done that is relevant, rather than a wall of former employers' logos. Explain how you divide responsibilities, who is full-time and which key hires this round will fund.

### The regulatory plan, for fintech and crypto

If you touch payments, lending, custody or tokens, regulation is part of the product. Show that you have mapped the rules in your launch markets, which licenses you need or access through partners, what your compliance program looks like and who owns it.

A credible regulatory plan is a competitive advantage, and ignoring the question is one of the fastest ways to lose our interest. For AI products, a short note on data rights, privacy and model risk does the same job.

### The raise and use of funds

State how much you are raising, the instrument if you know it, and what the money will accomplish. Tie the use of funds to milestones: the product you will ship, the customers or usage you will reach, the people you will hire and the runway that results.

The best version answers one question: what will be true at the end of this round that makes the next one straightforward? If the round includes token rights, say so plainly and explain the structure.

## Common mistakes

Weak decks tend to fail in a handful of predictable ways, and each is easy to fix once you see it.

- **Vanity metrics.** Cumulative sign-ups, social followers, wallet addresses and deposits attracted by incentives say little on their own. Show active usage, retention and revenue, and define what *active* means.
- **Leading with tokenomics.** A deck that opens with supply charts and allocation tables, before explaining the problem, tells us the token is the product. Lead with the business, and let the token earn its place later in the deck.
- **Very long decks.** If the core argument needs far more than a dozen slides, it is not yet sharp. Move supporting detail to an appendix.
- **Hiding risks.** Every company has them. Name the biggest ones and what you are doing about each. Founders who see their risks clearly are easier to back.
- **Jargon.** Acronyms, buzzwords and dense technical language hide weak thinking and lose readers outside your niche. Write so that a smart generalist understands every slide on first read.

## Format, and how to send it

Keep it short. Around a dozen slides is usually enough for the main argument, with an appendix for detail. The deck should read well without a voiceover, because the first time we see it, you will not be in the room. Give each slide one point, state that point in the headline and put the evidence underneath.

Share the deck as a PDF or a link. Make sure the link opens without an access request, and check that it reads well on a phone. Confirm that you are sending the latest version and that the figures agree from slide to slide. Small errors suggest bigger ones.
